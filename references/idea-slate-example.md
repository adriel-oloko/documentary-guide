# A real idea slate

Twelve subjects, tiered, with the reasoning. This came out of planning one channel
on the ships and shipwrecks family, and the shape of it is the point: four anchors,
six body, a deeper reserve, and two slots deliberately left empty.

Copy the structure, not the subjects.

## The rules the slate was built on

Extracted from the measured performance of a channel that had already published:
the winners were universally known subjects carrying a paradoxical or negative claim,
and the flops were either niche subjects or complimentary claims. The ships family
added one lever of its own: engineering forward episodes stay ad safe.

## Tier A, open with these

| # | Subject | The angle | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The Vasa (1628) | Sank after about a thousand three hundred metres on her maiden voyage, then sat in mud for three hundred and thirty three years and came up nearly whole | Absurd, universal, an engineering failure spine, no gore, no politics, ad safe |
| 2 | HMS Dreadnought | The ship that made every other battleship on earth obsolete in one afternoon | Pure machine biography, no casualties on screen |
| 3 | The Mary Celeste | Found under full sail, cargo intact, crew and lifeboat gone | The most famous unsolved maritime mystery there is, endless curiosity, no grimness |
| 4 | Project Azorian and the Glomar Explorer | An intelligence agency built a ship to raise a submarine from sixteen thousand feet behind a fake deep sea mining company | Spy story plus engineering plus absurdity, and almost nobody has done it properly |

Two of these worked out in full inside the pipeline. The Vasa episode's hook line is
real and is quoted below.

## Tier B, the body of the slate

| # | Subject | The angle | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | The Ever Given (2021) | One ship stopped twelve percent of world trade for six days | Modern, instantly recognizable, the only entry with a live search tail |
| 6 | The Edmund Fitzgerald (1975) | Twenty nine men, no survivors, no distress call, and a hit single | Huge seasonal spike every November, so schedule it for late October |
| 7 | USS Indianapolis | Delivered the first atomic bomb, was sunk, and the sharks took the rest | Very high recognition, carried by a famous monologue |
| 8 | The Halifax Explosion (1917) | Two ships collided and wiped out a city | City scale consequence, clear cause, engineering and cargo rather than a battle |
| 9 | The Franklin Expedition | One hundred and twenty nine men walked into the Arctic ice and vanished, then the wrecks were found | A modern discovery hook keeps it alive, and the testimony and the chemistry carry it without gore |

## Tier C, keep in reserve

Held until the channel has authority. Note the reason on each: this is where the
heaviest and strangest subjects sit, and some are held for taste rather than for
performance.

- The Wilhelm Gustloff. Nine thousand four hundred dead, the deadliest maritime
  disaster there is, and almost nobody outside one country knows it. The best paradox
  in the family and the heaviest subject in it.
- The Andrea Doria and the Stockholm collision.
- The Kursk, USS Thresher and USS Scorpion: submarines lost to their own design.
- The Hunley: first submarine to sink a warship, then vanished with her own crew.
- The Antikythera wreck and the mechanism.
- The Great Eastern: a commercial failure that laid the transatlantic cable.
- The Cutty Sark and the tea races.

## Slots eleven and twelve, kept open

Leave two of twelve unassigned. By episode eight there are real retention and click
numbers, and the correct eleventh and twelfth subjects are whichever Tier A entry
overperformed, cloned. Do not spend them on speculation.

## Do not do these

- **The most famous subject as an opener.** A decade of established coverage owns
  that keyword. If it must be done, do it late, with a specific non-generic angle: the
  two inquiries, or what the design got right for the next seventy years.
- **Generic "ships through history" listicles.** No paradox, no subject, no spine.
- **Current conflicts.** A monetisation landmine and an accuracy minefield.
- **Pure glamour subjects.** Modern cruise launches, flagship launches with nothing
  wrong with them. No failure, no story.
- **Deep designations** (obscure hull classes and refits) until there is an audience
  that will watch a designation.

## How one of these looked by the time it was written

The Vasa entry, worked through the five fields:

**Slug.** `vasa_1628`

**Subject.** The Swedish warship that heeled over and sank in her own harbour on her
maiden voyage in 1628, and came up nearly whole three centuries later.

**The angle.** Not a curse, not a single villain. A ship drawn for one gun deck,
built to a specification amended to two, that failed a stability test in front of
thirty witnesses in January, was not reported upward, and sailed seven months later.

**The hook line, as written.** "On the tenth of August, in the year sixteen twenty
eight, the largest warship Sweden had ever built slipped her moorings in Stockholm
harbour, spread her sails, and set out on the first voyage of her life."

**The correction.** Everyone believes an ancient ship sank because of a storm or bad
luck. The record shows she failed a stability test before she sailed, the officer who
ran the test said so out loud, no one wrote it down, and the king's own order for the
second deck of heavy guns was the reason she was unstable. The inquest the following
autumn concluded that she had been built to the royal specification by men following
orders, and nobody was punished.

**Tier.** A. Ad safety: perfect. No casualties on screen, the sea and the hull carry
the whole film, and the lesson is portable.

**Risk.** The loss beat carries a death toll. Handle it at document distance: the
people aboard, the number, and the fact that the water was shallow enough to wade.

That is the level of detail a slate entry needs before it is promoted. A tier and a
subject line are not enough to start writing from.
