1. On the tenth of August, in the year sixteen twenty eight, the largest warship Sweden had ever built slipped
2. her moorings in Stockholm harbour, spread her sails, and set out on the first voyage of her life.
3. She was called the Vasa, named for the royal house that had ordered her,
4. and she had been built for one purpose. The Baltic was to become a Swedish sea.
5. She carried sixty four guns on two complete decks. Her stern was carved with lions and emperors
6. and crowned by a gallery three storeys high, and every figure on her was painted
7. in colours bright enough to be read from the shore. Measured against anything afloat in northern Europe that morning,
8. she was the most powerful ship in the water, and the most beautiful.
9. She travelled about a thousand three hundred metres. Then a gust came down off the hills and took her.
10. She heeled to larboard, further than a hull of her size had any business heeling,
11. and for a few seconds she hung there, held by wind on one side and ballast on the other.
12. The men on deck had time to understand exactly what was about to happen to them.
13. Her lower gun ports were open, because a ship cleared for action sails with her ports open,
14. and the Baltic came in through thirty openings cut for thirty heavy guns.
15. She filled, rolled onto her side, and went under in minutes, in water shallow enough
16. that her masts stayed above the surface to mark the place where she lay.
17. The dead are usually given as somewhere between thirty and fifty, out of about a hundred and fifty aboard.
18. That is the famous part, and it is not why the Vasa still matters.
19. Start with why the ship existed at all. Sweden in the sixteen twenties was a poor country
20. with an ambitious king, and it was fighting on several fronts at once.
21. Gustav Adolf had watched his navy fail him, and the Baltic was the highway his armies
22. and his grain and his iron all travelled along. Whoever controlled that water controlled the war.
23. Denmark held the narrow passages at the mouth of it, Poland contested the southern shore,
24. and the Dutch and the English traded through it whenever they liked. A fleet
25. that could not be challenged would change all of that, and a fleet begins with one ship
26. that makes every other ship obsolete. The king wanted that ship to be enormous.
27. The master shipwright, a Dutchman named Henrik Hybertsson, drew a hull with a single gun deck
28. and a length he considered already ambitious. The king doubled the armament on paper before the keel was even
29. laid, and then sent word that the ship should carry a second full deck of heavy guns as well.
30. Hybertsson had an impossible problem in front of him. A hull has a fixed width,
31. and every gun added high up on that width makes the whole thing less stable.
32. He built what he was told to build, and he died before the ship was finished,
33. leaving the work to his assistant. The result was a hull carrying more weight above the waterline than any
34. ship of her size had ever carried, with far too little ballast below it.
35. She was wide, but not wide enough for what had been piled on top of her.
36. Every carpenter who worked on her had spent a lifetime learning what a stable ship looks like,
37. and the Vasa was not one. Everything about the armament made the problem worse.
38. The lower deck carried the heaviest guns in the ship, and they sat barely clear of the waterline,
39. so that any roll of more than a few degrees put them under.
40. The upper deck carried the same weight again, higher up, on a hull whose beam had been settled months
41. before anyone asked what it would have to bear. The yard was running at speed, with hundreds of men,
42. Dutch and Swedish, against a schedule set by a war, and the timber had been cut
43. and shaped long before anybody had a reason to recalculate what the finished ship was going to weigh.
44. What came off the slip was a hull that had been built correctly to a plan that was wrong,
45. and no single person in the yard was in a position to say so.
46. The testing came before the launch, and it is the strangest part of the story.
47. In January sixteen twenty eight, with the hull still unrigged, thirty men were ordered to run from one side
48. of the deck to the other, back and forth, to see how far she would roll.
49. After three runs the ship was leaning so far that they stopped, and the officer
50. in charge called a halt rather than risk losing her at the quay.
51. That test had told everyone present the truth. The ship was dangerously tender.
52. Nobody wrote it down, and nobody acted on it, and seven months later she was loaded
53. with guns and stores and sent to sea. The order to sail came from the crown.
54. The admiral in command had spent the morning visiting the fleet, and he was not aboard,
55. which is why he lived. The ship left in a light breeze, and the breeze was all it took.
56. When she went down, the water was so close to the shore that boats were alongside within minutes,
57. and the men who rescued survivors were hauling people out of a harbour they could have swum across.
58. Some of the people on board were trapped below, and some were the families of crew,
59. women and children who had been allowed aboard for a short trip on a calm day.
60. An inquest was held that autumn inside the palace. It heard witnesses,
61. it heard that the ship had been run through a stability test and failed it,
62. and it concluded that the ship had been built to the crown's own specification by men who were following
63. orders. No one was executed. No one was fined. The crown's own instructions were the reason the ship was
64. unstable, and the court had no interest in establishing that on the record.
65. The most powerful person in the room had ordered the thing that killed a hundred
66. and fifty of his subjects, and the paperwork was arranged so that
67. this could not be said out loud. Then she was forgotten. Not completely,
68. because her masts stood out of the water as a hazard for the harbour, and they were cut away,
69. and for three centuries she was a note in a dredging survey and little else.
70. That three centuries is the other half of the story, and it is a matter of chemistry.
71. The Baltic is brackish and cold, and it does not host the shipworm
72. that eats wooden shipwrecks everywhere else in the world. A wooden hull on the seabed
73. of almost any other sea is gone within a generation. The Vasa lay in mud
74. and low salinity on an even keel, and slowly filled with sediment, and was preserved almost whole.
75. A naval officer and amateur researcher named Anders Franzen spent years hunting for her,
76. and located her in August nineteen fifty six, in a spot close
77. to the island where a young man had once rowed out to test a home made sounding line.
78. He had been probing the harbour bed for years with a core sampler and
79. a grappling hook, on a hunch that the water would have protected what
80. it had taken. The raising took years and nearly failed. Baby steps first:
81. tunnelling under the hull with water jets to pass cables, then lifting her
82. from one shallower position to the next, a few metres at a time,
83. until she could be floated into a dry dock in nineteen sixty one.
84. Nobody had recovered a big timber hull in this condition before and nobody knew how it would behave out
85. of the water. It began to crack and shrink almost as soon as it started to dry,
86. and the problem of conserving her has never fully ended. Her crew were found with her.
87. Excavations recovered clothing, food, coins, tools, chests, and the remains of at least seventeen people,
88. and the bones answered questions the paperwork never did. The conservation has never actually finished.
89. The hull sits on a cradle under controlled humidity, and the treatment
90. that stopped the waterlogged timber from collapsing as it dried took close to two decades,
91. followed by years of slow settling during which the ship moved, shrank and had
92. to be supported all over again. Seen from the gallery today she reads less like a wreck than like
93. a building under repair that has never closed, which is exactly what she is.
94. Visitors walk past the gun decks at eye level and can see the ports that drowned her,
95. cut low in the hull, still open. What the Vasa gave naval architecture is a control case.
96. Every rule about stability in a wooden hull is easier to accept once a complete example
97. of the failure is standing in a museum, on a cradle,
98. with the gun decks open and the ballast still in the bilge where it was found.
99. What she gave everyone else is a smaller and more portable lesson. The information needed
100. to prevent the disaster was in the room months before she sailed. It was known, measured,
101. and then set aside, because the person who needed to hear it was the person who had caused it.
